# The Frame Needs a Reason: Why Generative Imagery Has to Be Directed, Not Just Decorated

*objekts / Notes on visual authorship · 26 October 2026 · English*

Generative images have made it remarkably easy to create an expensive-looking frame. They have made it no easier to explain why that frame belongs at that exact moment in a film. A plausible ray of sunlight, a handsome lens flare and rich surface detail can mask an image without a point of view. Production is then reduced to a hunt for attractive accidents.

We have found a more demanding question useful: **what must the viewer discover in this frame, and what is the minimum physical arrangement required for that discovery to happen?**

That question begins in directing, not in a model selector.

## A space is not a backdrop

A room has exits, obstacles, light sources and a history of actions. A camera has an axis and a height; it does not teleport to an invented reverse angle without consequences. A person has mass, joints and habitual gestures. Fabric has tension, friction and lag. Once those properties are treated as arbitrary, every new angle feels like a new universe.

That is why our image work separates an empty location, a character study, wardrobe and props before combining them into a shot. The separate stages aren't a bureaucratic ritual. They make failures visible. If the camera geometry is wrong, fix the space. If the facial proportions are wrong, fix identity. If a jacket looks like painted plastic, investigate the cloth rather than rewriting the whole city.

A single sentence—“cinematic, beautifully lit, realistic”—contains almost no usable information about any of those problems.

## The drama of an ordinary object

Imagine a figure entering a shop, reaching for a metal trolley and moving it slightly. The action is almost nothing. But the frame can turn on whether a viewer sees contact before the trolley moves, whether the hand takes the load, whether the wheels turn in the direction of travel, whether the reflection on the metal belongs to the actual light above it.

If these events are coherent, the mundane object suddenly has dramatic presence. If they are not, even extraordinary scenery looks hollow. Generative imagery often fails not because it lacks visual richness, but because its surfaces are disconnected from causes.

An art director is therefore deciding relationships—material to light, body to object, foreground to background—not merely decorating individual pixels.

## References should argue, not vote

A pile of ten images is not ten pieces of evidence for one look. Each may be a different argument: one about quiet acting, one about window light, one about a fabric, one about camera distance. If you let all of them vote on the whole scene, their unrelated actors and spaces begin to leak into yours.

Our reference cards make the argument explicit: *this source controls the ratio of practical light to ambient light; it must not control the room*. Or: *this one shows the physical weight of a heavy coat; it must not change the performer's face*.

The result is less magical and more deliberate. That is a useful trade.

## A series is a world, not a gallery

The task of a coherent film is different from the task of making its best still. A film demands that the next frame remember the last. The door stays where it was. The cup remains on the table unless someone moved it. The performer can change expression without changing anatomy. A camera move reveals new facts about the same place.

This principle also shapes our independent visual experiments. In [The Invisible Silhouette](https://objekts.ai/en/work/fashion-in-motion), garments function as moving bodies in ordinary city spaces. The project is most interesting when architecture, a shadow or the implied weight of fabric gives an absent figure a credible presence. The premise is surreal; the surrounding world needs an exact physical logic.

The work is not an argument that realism should make all images look like photography. It is an argument that material and spatial truth can make an impossible image readable.

## A better definition of a successful generation

A generation job that returns a file has completed computation. It has not necessarily completed an artistic task. A producer must be able to reject a beautiful frame because the actor has become somebody else, a prop moves before contact or the camera breaks a decided axis.

We record failures alongside successful candidates because failed attempts reveal the hidden rules of a scene. “Wrong shadow direction” is a better diagnosis than “not cinematic enough.” “A lighting reference moved the doorway” is something we can repair. An unexamined dislike is not.

## What we want to publish

We are making our production notes public in a limited, useful form: reference provenance, causal motion, scene continuity, frame-accurate shot planning, and the gap between model completion and human approval. These are not advertisements disguised as tutorials. The goal is to make production knowledge inspectable enough that another director, designer or software agent can challenge it, reproduce the logic and improve it.

The protected parts of a project remain protected: client briefs, unreleased boards, contributor and likeness rights, raw reference files. The transferable part is the thinking.

An image becomes authored when someone can explain not only how it was made, but why its geometry, light and timing could not be arbitrary.

# Give the Shot the Time It Needs: Duration as Production Design
**objekts Field Notes · FN-26 · English · AI-assisted editorial draft · 26 October 2026**

A shot can contain a body, a window, a remarkable surface and a precise light source and still possess no duration. It survives as an isolated image. The moment it begins moving, the filmmaker has no idea when it should end.

Generative-video workflows make this problem deceptively easy to conceal. A model can fill an assigned number of seconds with camera movement, drifting weather or fabric simulation. A client sees ten seconds of activity. But does the viewer learn anything at second seven that was not present at second one? Is the time an event needs different from the time an interface happens to provide?

The central proposal is simple: **the duration of a shot is an art-direction decision**. It is built from waiting, observation, gesture, offscreen pressure, camera position and the relation to the next shot. Texture can support duration. It cannot replace it.

The discussion refers to artists’ own published accounts of their filmmaking and to a fictional teaching sequence. It makes no claim that our example was filmed, generated, approved or exhibited.

## 1. What duration does that composition cannot

Composition establishes a field of possible attention. Duration determines which of those possibilities the viewer actually lives through.

Consider an empty kitchen with a rectangular fluorescent practical above a sink. A pair of rubber gloves hangs over the tap. On the left edge of the composition, a narrow doorway opens into darkness. Nothing visibly happens.

At 0.7 seconds, this might be a location establishing image. At four seconds, the viewer begins to inspect the doorway. At eleven, the unoccupied space may become a subject rather than background. At twenty, the image risks becoming oppressive or strangely comic, depending on sound and context. These are interpretive possibilities, not measured universal audience reactions.

The director must decide what the frame is supposed to make possible:

- Recognize the geography?
- See that someone is absent?
- Anticipate a movement from the doorway?
- Listen to an event behind the wall?
- Have enough time to interpret a face?
- Experience a real, difficult wait?

Each goal implies a different end condition. If the goal is mere geographic recognition, a long hold may be redundant. If the subject is waiting, a quick cut can destroy the subject completely.

## 2. Pedro Costa: time is part of the means of production

In a first-person interview about *Vitalina Varela*, Pedro Costa connects his working methods to months spent with collaborators, the ownership of basic filmmaking tools and the time required for a person, a shot and a structure to emerge. [BFI / Sight and Sound, *Living memory: Pedro Costa on Vitalina Varela*, 2020](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/interviews/living-memory-pedro-costa-vitalina-varela).

This is not a direct technical instruction for AI advertising, and it should not be quoted as an endorsement of generative imagery. The useful comparison is about the limits of speed. A new image can arrive in seconds. A filmmaker still has to discover what people and objects do inside it.

There are two different things called iteration:

1. Requesting a different image from a generator.
2. Looking long enough at a particular image to understand what is wrong or necessary.

The first is easy to accelerate. The second involves attention, judgment and often collaboration. An efficient production has to budget for both, though not on the same scale as Costa's cinema.

## 3. Albert Serra: the camera should remain available to what happens

In an interview with BFI, Albert Serra discusses lightweight digital cameras and the value of keeping technical intrusion low so that events and performances can unfold in front of the lens. [BFI / Sight and Sound, *“Against, against”: creative destruction with Albert Serra*, 2015](https://www.bfi.org.uk/sight-and-sound/interviews/against-against-creative-destruction-with-albert-serra).

That position can be read as a challenge to a generative production habit: writing all the imagined image behavior in advance and asking the machine to enact an aesthetically complete miniature. If every gesture, dolly move and effect is prescribed, the image may have no room for an unanticipated rhythm.

It would be dishonest to claim that a generative system improvises with the human agency of Serra's collaborators. It does not. The methodological lesson is narrower: plan spaces and situations rather than only ornamented outcomes. Give the actor or constructed object a specific physical task. Inspect the result for accidental but useful timing. Do not lock every variable simply because it can be put into a prompt.

## 4. A fictional three-shot sequence

A worker enters a half-empty tailoring room before dawn. A coat hangs from a high hook. A small lamp stands beside a cutting table; the window is still almost black. The worker intends to leave, hesitates, then returns to touch the coat.

The sequence can be built three ways.

**Version A: information-first.** Wide room, entrance, hand on coat, exit. Every shot exists to deliver an item of plot. Duration is compressed. This might be correct for an explainer where clarity is everything.

**Version B: suspicion-first.** The room is held empty. A noise suggests the worker in a neighboring space. The worker enters too late to explain the sound. The hand lingers on the coat. Nothing supernatural occurs. Duration concentrates attention on the ambiguity of the object and the person’s motive.

**Version C: tactile-first.** Cut directly to the sleeve and hand. A longer contact reveals resistance in the fabric, the worker’s interrupted breath and an almost unconscious adjustment of the hook. A later wide shot tells us where we are. Duration is earned by touch and delayed geography.

All three can use the same room, wardrobe, lens kit and lighting canon. They have distinct dramatic meanings because time and order changed. A prompt that merely says “slow cinematic movement” does not decide among them.

## 5. Map shots by changes of knowledge

A useful edit sheet separates what the camera shows from what the audience comes to know.

| Shot | Initial visible information | Event in time | What changes for the viewer | Appropriate exit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 01, empty wide | Unoccupied room, coat, lamp, door | Distant footsteps from another space | Someone will enter; coat becomes a question | After the expectation is established, before it turns inert |
| 02, person enters | Figure crosses doorway and stops | Their eyeline finds the coat before the camera moves | The object has a private meaning | After their hesitation becomes legible |
| 03, detail | Cloth, hand, hook | Fingers test the weight; small fabric correction | Connection is embodied, not explained | After the gesture completes and its consequence registers |

No universal frame count is attached. Frame counts should be assigned during animatic, performance rehearsal or generated-motion tests and revised against actual results. The question is not “how many seconds should a poetic shot last?” The question is “what is the last instant this shot must contain?”

## 6. The material clock

Every material carries a pace. Water falls differently from velvet. An old hinge does not necessarily close at the same speed as a weightless visual effect. A heavy sleeve takes time to rise, pull on a shoulder and settle. A face takes time to turn toward a sound, but even a small turn contains acceleration and reaction.

For production, write a **material clock** beside each physical action:

- **Trigger:** what applies force, or what prompts the person.
- **Latency:** how long before the object or body visibly responds.
- **Rise:** speed and direction of the action.
- **Contact:** interruption, collision or weight transfer.
- **Decay:** settling, resistance, tremor, breathing.
- **Read:** how long the viewer needs to register consequence.

This structure differs from writing “slow motion.” A slow physical action can feel fast if its meaning is immediate. A rapid action can create a long psychological aftereffect if the shot holds on the changed space.

In generative video, the failure mode is a velocity-ambiguous object: cloth begins moving before the body touches it; the hand crosses into the garment without impact; texture settles while its support continues moving. These are causality failures, not simply aesthetic preferences. They require specific frame review, not more adjectives.

## 7. Camera movement as a second clock

If the camera pushes forward, its motion creates a schedule of revelations. Foreground elements pass by at different rates. Occlusion opens or closes relationships. A person may enter the edge of the composition not because they moved, but because the camera crossed a threshold.

Define a camera movement by geometry:

- starting pose, height and aim;
- lens/FOV, format and crop;
- intended path and speed;
- relevant depth layers;
- points where an object first becomes visible, partially obscured or fully revealed;
- endpoint and whether motion actually stops.

Then ask: is that movement necessary at all? A locked camera can keep the viewer searching among possibilities. A slow push may tell the viewer where to look, prematurely ending that search. The decision is dramatic, not a generic preference for handheld or stabilized images.

**A camera move should not be used merely to prove that the image is video.**

## 8. The sound clock

Offscreen sound can start the shot before its visible subject arrives. It can also extend the shot after the visible action ends. In the tailoring room, footsteps in the corridor may be more decisive than the entrance. The soft motion of cloth may be more charged after the hand retreats.

Build the sound clock separately:

1. Room presence before any character.
2. First offscreen cue.
3. Listener's physical reaction.
4. Onscreen source confirmation, if it occurs.
5. Tail or silence after visible completion.

When picture and audio have independent schedules, the editor can make a deliberate decision about where to cut. Audio that ends precisely with every shot boundary creates an artificially compartmentalized world. An abrupt cut can be right, but it should be intentional.

## 9. Decision gates for a generative workflow

**Gate 1 — Statement.** What is the shot’s dramatic operation? If answer is only “show premium visuals,” stop and clarify.

**Gate 2 — World.** Which physical and visual attributes are immutable? Person, wardrobe, place topology, lens and light source must not drift while waiting for an action.

**Gate 3 — Beats.** Mark specific changes of knowledge or physical state, not a list of camera adjectives.

**Gate 4 — Timing.** Assign approximate seconds and frames, then refine after motion tests. Generator capability and output length limits are version-specific and must be checked against the real interface.

**Gate 5 — Physical QC.** Inspect entry, acceleration, contact, release, occlusion and settling; reject impossible motion even if the still frames are beautiful.

**Gate 6 — Edit context.** Evaluate the shot with its predecessor and successor, and with intended sound. A hold that works in isolation may repeat information the previous shot already gave.

**Gate 7 — Approval.** Keep creative approval separate from generation job completion, machine-level diagnostics or project-management status.

A practical treatment should include an **exit criterion** per shot: cut when the hand releases, when offscreen sound becomes identifiable, when the viewer understands the doorway's significance, or when an action's aftermath can no longer change the next shot. “Cut at 4.5 seconds because this model outputs 4.5 seconds” is a service constraint, not an artistic reason.

## 10. Duration and commercial storytelling are compatible

None of this argues that a commercial should be slow. A short spot may demand ruthless compression. The lesson is that compression should remove the intervals that do not contribute, rather than reducing every gesture to a synthetic midpoint.

A 15-second film has very little room for empty decoration. Every action needs a causal beginning and enough time for a viewer to understand it. In a longer brand film, the same discipline allows deliberate quiet. In both, duration is designed, rehearsed or iterated rather than borrowed from a generator’s interface.

The market does not need a second-by-second formula for “cinematic.” It needs editors, art directors and production engineers who can argue precisely why a frame remains, moves, or disappears.

## 11. A production handoff: duration ledger

For each shot, store:

- `shot_id`, narrative role, runtime/fps and actual delivered frame count;
- start/end camera pose and approved optic;
- geography and lighting master reference, irreversible LOCKs;
- actor/prop positions, gaze and motion state;
- sound event cue sheet, including offscreen antecedents and tails;
- visual beats and frame ranges;
- material-clock trigger, contact and settle events;
- editorial entry/exit criterion, previous/next shot dependency;
- technical evidence (actual render path and frame review) vs subjective art approval;
- revisions expressed as exact deltas, not unbounded “more cinema”.

Do not invent receipts or over-promise exact frame control from a generator. When the tool cannot meet the ledger, change the production method, adjust the sequence or escalate the creative decision.
